                                                                                 Abstract
                                                     We give an extensive list of parametrized families of polynomial con-
                                                 tinued fractions of smallest possible degrees for π 2 and ζ(3), and mention
                                                 similar results for other constants.



                                           1     Introduction
                                           One can find in the literature a huge number of continued fractions (abbreviated
                                           CFs) for interesting constants and functions. The purpose of this paper is
                                           to give possibly experimental results showing that there often exist families
                                           of such CFs, usually depending on two, three, and even four parameters. In
                                           many cases these experimental results can be proved, usually because either
                                           pn /qn or pn /qn − pn−1 /qn−1 have an explicit expression. In other cases, I have
                                           not bothered to try to prove the experimental results, although it may not be
                                           difficult. Thus, when I speak of results, they should be considered experimental,
                                           but with considerable evidence. I would appreciate receiving any additional data
                                           of the same kind.
                                               Notation: a CF will be written as a pair of sequences (an , bn )n≥0 represent-
                                           ing the CF a0 + b0 /(a1 + b1 /(a2 + b2 /(a3 + · · · ))) (warning: note this precise
                                           convention, other authors have different notation). All the CFs that we will
                                           encounter will be convergent : if as usual pn /qn = a0 + b0 /(a1 + · · · + bn−1 /an ),
                                           this means that z = limn→∞ pn /qn exists, and when we speak of the speed of
                                           convergence, we mean the behavior of z − pn /qn .
                                               Note that we always assume that bn 6= 0 for all n, but we allow a finite
                                           number of an to vanish.

Definition 1.1 (1) Let (an )n≥0 be a sequence (always assumed to be of ratio-
     nal numbers, and usually of integers). We say that it is ultimately poly-
     nomial if there exists a polynomial denoted a(x) (by abuse of notation)
     with rational coefficients such that an = a(n) and an 6= 0 for n sufficiently
     large.
 (2) We say that the CF (an , bn )n≥0 is of polynomial type if the sequences an
     and bn are ultimately polynomial.
    As a practical notation, if an = a(n) for n ≥ n0 , we will denote the sequence
(an )n≥0 by the (n0 + 1)-component vector A = (a0 , a1 , . . . , an0 −1 , a(n)), where
the last entry is a polynomial. Of course this is not unique: we also have for
instance A = (a0 , a1 , . . . , an0 −1 , an0 , a(n)) with an0 = a(n0 ).
    Thus a CF will be denoted by a pair of such vectors A and B representing
(an ) and (bn ), and if the CF converges to some number z, we will write z =
(an , bn ).
Definition 1.2 We recall that two CFs (an , bn ) and (a′n , b′n ) are said to be
equivalent if there exists an integer k such that (a′n , b′n ) = (an+k , bn+k ) for n
sufficiently large, i.e., if the tails of the CFs are ultimately the same.
   Since we assume everything rational, if we set z = (an , bn ) and z ′ = (a′n , b′n ),
the CFs for z and z ′ are equivalent if and only if there exists a Möbius trans-
formation with integer coefficients sending z to z ′ , in other words z ′ = (Az +
B)/(Cz + D) for integers A, B, C, D with AD − BC 6= 0.
   We can now give our final definition:
Definition 1.3 We say that a pair of polynomials (A(x), B(x)) with rational
coefficients represents a constant z if there exists a CF (an , bn ) converging to z
such that an = A(n) and bn = B(n) for n sufficiently large, and in that case we
simply write by abuse of notation z = (A(n), B(n)).
    Because of what we have said above, this is almost, but not quite, equivalent
to the fact that the CF (A(n), B(n))n≥0 converges to a Möbius transform of z
(always assumed with integer coefficients): indeed, some values of A(n) and/or
B(n) may vanish, which kills this property. But if we avoid these vanishing
values by changing the corresponding coefficients of the CF to any nonzero
value, this is indeed true.
    Our goal is, given some interesting constant z, to find all (or at least many)
pairs (A, B) representing z, subject to suitable restrictions. In particular, recall
that if (an , bn )n≥0 is a CF converging to z and rn is any sequence with r0 = 1
and rn 6= 0 for all n, the CF (an rn , bn rn rn+1 )n≥0 also converges to z. We will
thus restrict to polynomials (A, B) of smallest possible degrees.
    To make this more precise, we introduce the following definitions:
Definition 1.4 We say that z ∈ C is a rational P period of degree k if it is equal
to the sum of a convergent series of the form       n≥1 χ(n)f (n), where χ is a
periodic arithmetic function taking rational values, and f ∈ Q(x) is a rational
function with rational coefficients whose denominator is of degree k.

2     CFs for π 2 : Convergence in 1/nP , P ∈ Z≥1
As far as the author is aware, up to equivalence there exist exactly nine types of
convergence of polynomial continued fractions for π 2 with bidegree ≤ (2, 4): two
families with convergence in 1/nP with P ∈ Z, one with convergence in 1/nP
with P ∈ 1/2 + Z, one with convergence in (−1)n /nP , one with convergence
1/(E n nP ) for each of E = 4/3, 2, 4, and −8,
                                            √and finally the unique CF of Apéry
with convergence in 1/E n for E = −((1 + 5)/2)10 . We consider each of these
families in turn.
